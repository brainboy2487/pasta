The Deterministic Generalization of Markov Chains: A Framework for Stochastic Tensor Computation with Bounded Error and Auditable Reduction (The MAGE Paradigm)
1. Introduction and Motivation: From Stochastic Matrices to N-way Arrays
1.1 The Transition from Matrices to Stochastic Tensors (Higher-Order Markov Chains)
Classical Markov Chains (MCs) are founded upon the premise that the probability of the next state, X_{t+1}, depends only on the current state, X_t. This dependence is mathematically governed by a 2D transition matrix, P \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}, which must satisfy the conditions of a stochastic matrix (non-negative elements and rows summing to one). This structure facilitates standard Markov semigroup theory and analysis of long-term behavior.
However, modeling complex, real-world sequential processes—such as those encountered in multi-agent systems or advanced context-aware AI—often necessitates capturing dependencies on multiple previous states or incorporating external covariates. This requirement forces the mathematical generalization of the 2D matrix P into a higher-order stochastic tensor T, often referred to as an N-way array where N \ge 3. These structures are central to Higher-Order Markov Chains (HMCs). The MAGE system (Markov Answer Generation Engine) explicitly formalizes its computation around this principle, defining its core operation as dD contraction across these stochastic tensors. Theoretical research confirms that the existence of key characteristics for HMCs, such as the limiting (stationary) probability distribution, relies on establishing sufficient conditions based on the regularity of this transition tensor, thereby extending the conclusions previously limited to first-order chains. Moreover, practical applications, particularly in bioinformatics and image processing, have historically demonstrated that higher-order HMMs (Hidden Markov Models) can provide substantial predictive improvements over their first-order counterparts.
1.2 The Challenge of Scalability: The Curse of Dimensionality and Tractability
While mathematically rigorous, the adoption of higher-order stochastic tensors immediately confronts the curse of dimensionality. For a system with n states and dependence on d previous states, a dense transition tensor can scale exponentially, requiring memory and computational resources proportional to O(n^{2+d}). MAGE's dense contraction cost model estimates the overall complexity as O(n^2 K) elements touched across K slices. This exponential scaling confirms that full, dense computation is not only computationally expensive but also highly impractical for production systems, often rendering classical solvers infeasible for models such as those used in cancer progression analysis.
Acknowledging this limitation, MAGE identifies that in high-performance computing (HPC) environments, memory bandwidth, rather than raw floating-point operations (FLOPs), often dominates the time required for dense contraction. This realization constrains the system design to prioritize minimizing the movement of bytes via techniques like block generation and parameter storage in the cold tier. To overcome the scalability bottleneck, a design philosophy must be adopted that replaces dense, bandwidth-heavy operations with memory-efficient, mathematically equivalent approximations.
1.3 Defining Auditable Computation: The Mandate for Determinism and Error Control
The requirement for achieving a "lossless projection" of Markov Chains to higher dimensions, combined with the explicit mandate to aggressively double-check and eliminate error propagation, necessitates a fundamental shift in definition. Since the curse of dimensionality dictates that full dense computation is intractable, the system must introduce approximation. Therefore, MAGE must reconcile the concept of "lossless" with "approximation."
The solution is the realization of a Controlled-Loss Projection framework. Within this framework, "lossless" is redefined to mean two mathematically enforceable conditions:
 * Bit-wise numerical determinism: Ensuring zero numerical error introduced by non-associative floating-point operations or dynamic parallel scheduling.
 * Mathematically bounded approximation error: Guaranteeing that the deviation from the idealized dense calculation is analytically bounded by quantifiable metrics, such as the per-row L1 omission bound (\|P - \hat{P}\|_1 \le W_{\text{om}}).
MAGE codifies this philosophy through its system design principles: demanding Determinism and Auditability (identical inputs yield identical outputs), enforcing Predictable Latency via per-step operations budgets (Rule 42), and maintaining Formal Error Control. This distinction is critical; MAGE transforms the problem from an infeasible search for dense exactitude into a tractable problem of quantifiable, bounded error.
The inherent trade-offs between dimensionality, complexity, and error control are summarized below, illustrating how MAGE transitions from a theoretical matrix representation to an operational stochastic tensor framework.

To maintain the fundamental Markov property—the conservation of probability mass—the resulting matrix P must be rigorously stochastic. MAGE enforces per-row normalization: s_i = \sum_{j} P_{ij}. If s_i > 0, the probabilities are scaled, P_{ij} \leftarrow P_{ij}/s_i. In the rare event of a row sum s_i \le 0 (e.g., due to catastrophic underflow or numerical error), a deterministic uniform distribution fallback (1/n) is enforced to guarantee continuous system operation and non-negativity.
The weighting mechanism W_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}} is crucial for tractability. MAGE supports highly structured weights, including separable factors (W_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}} = \prod f^{(a)}(\alpha_a)), power laws, exponential kernels, and weighted mixtures potentially incorporating attention mechanisms. Separable weights are particularly important because they align mathematically with low-rank tensor decompositions, which is essential for transforming the exponential complexity of the Cold and Warm generator tiers into a polynomial one.
2.2 Operator-Theoretic Framing and Evolution
The stochastic tensor contraction allows MAGE to model the dynamics of the system within the rigorous context of Markov semigroup theory. The transition process is governed by the semigroup (P_t)_{t \ge 0}, ensuring that transitions remain compositional via the generalized Chapman–Kolmogorov equation: P_{t+s} = P_t P_s. This fundamental property permits the reliable modeling of multi-step transitions via sequential tensor contractions.
The evolution of system state is formally linked to the infinitesimal generator L, where the transition operator is defined, at least formally, as P_t = e^{tL}. The forward and backward evolution equations are specified as \partial_t u(t,x) = L u(t,x) and \partial_t p(t,x,y) = L^{*}_y p(t,x,y), respectively. This framework confirms MAGE's capability to accurately model transient distributions—probabilities that are far from stationary—which is vital for time-sensitive tasks like tumor progression modeling where the time of observation is unknown.
### 2.3 Stationary Distributions and Ergodicity Diagnostics
A critical component of Markov system analysis is determining the long-term behavior. For Higher-Order Markov Chains, the existence of a limiting probability distribution is established by conditions on the regularity of the transition tensor, which extends the conclusions applicable to first-order chains.
The rate at which the system converges to this equilibrium (mixing time) is quantified by the spectral gap, defined as 1 - \lambda_2, where \lambda_2 is the magnitude of the second-largest eigenvalue of the transition matrix P. A smaller spectral gap indicates slower mixing, which in turn implies a longer transient behavior.
This concept of system mixing is intrinsically linked to MAGE’s operational efficiency. The TAP parser implements a confidence metric \Delta = P_{i j^{\ast}} - P_{i j^{(2)}} (the difference between the top two probabilities in a row) to trigger an early commit if \Delta exceeds a threshold \tau, enforcing a bounded latency regime. If the spectral gap is small, the current distribution P(t) is far from stationarity (slow mixing). Relying solely on a high instantaneous local confidence \Delta might lead the system to commit to an unstable state that, while certain now, is highly likely to change dramatically in subsequent steps.
Therefore, the proper operation of MAGE requires dynamic, rather than fixed, control. The confidence threshold \tau must be calibrated against real-time diagnostics of the system's global ergodicity. This elevates the TAP confidence calibration from a simple engineering heuristic to a problem in rigorous dynamic control theory, similar to those found in stochastic target problems. MAGE's operational stability relies on implementing empirical ergodicity diagnostics to inform the adaptive tuning of \tau [ (Issues)].
3. Representational Tractability: Tensor Network Factorizations
3.1 Tensor Decompositions as Loss-Controlled Encoders
The only viable engineering solution to the curse of dimensionality, while maintaining high-dimensional structure, is the use of Tensor Networks (TNs). TNs are versatile data structures that compactly represent large tensors, fundamentally converting exponential complexity into polynomial complexity. This representation is achieved via decomposition methods that express the high-order tensor as a contraction of multiple, lower-order tensors of low rank.
MAGE’s architecture leverages these established methodologies:
 * CP (Canonical Polyadic) Decomposition: This decomposes a tensor as a sum of rank-one tensors. This aligns with MAGE's use of separable factors in the Warm and Cold tiers, which are highly efficient kernels for stochastic tensor contraction.
 * Tucker Decomposition: An extension of Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to higher dimensions, utilizing a core tensor and basis matrices.
However, the power of TNs rests on the truncation of low singular values, meaning that decomposition is inherently an approximation technique. This SVD truncation is the mechanism that introduces bounded loss in the MAGE system. The benefit is immense: by increasing the bond dimension cutoff (\chi), the statistical error can be shown to decrease exponentially.
3.2 Matrix Product States (MPS) / Tensor Train (TT) for Probabilistic Modeling
For sequential and time-series data, the Matrix Product State (MPS), also known as the Tensor Train (TT) decomposition, is the most robust TN structure. This factorization expresses an N-index tensor as a chain-like product of 3-index tensors. This specific structure is highly effective for probabilistic sequence modeling.
Crucially, research has established profound mathematical equivalence results between the uniform variants of these quantum tensor network models (like MPS Born machines) and models found in stochastic process literature (such as Predictive State Representations and Hidden Markov Models). This equivalence validates MAGE's core premise: the high-dimensional Markov modeling it performs is mathematically consistent with proven, robust probabilistic sequence models.
3.3 MAGE Tiered Generator Operational Properties
MAGE’s tiered generator architecture—Hot, Warm, and Cold—is an operationalization of a dynamic Tensor Network contraction engine. The system dynamically selects the optimal TN representation (from full rank to low-rank factorization) based on real-time factors like weight mass, residual error, and recency of access. The performance targets (ns/element) reflect the varying costs of executing these different TN kernels.
The Hot tier, optimized for high-weight slices, uses contiguous dense doubles for block extraction, boasting a low latency of approximately 0.66 ns/element. The primary performance limiting factor here is memory bandwidth, demanding cache-aware blocking.
The Warm tier utilizes low-rank factorizations (U V^\top or separable factors) tuned by rank r to achieve a balance between accuracy and cost, with an estimated latency of 7.1 ns/element. The efficiency of this tier is critically dependent on meticulous memory layout. MAGE mandates that the V factor be transposed and packed row-major to ensure contiguous memory reads and maximize the utilization of Single Instruction, Multiple Data (SIMD) vectorization, which is crucial for maximizing throughput and meeting the latency budget [ (Issues)]. Without these memory optimizations, the computational gains offered by low-rank factorization would be negated by inefficient memory movement.
The Cold tier, reserved for analytic or low-activity slices, utilizes separable polynomials or fixed-point tables, with a much higher latency of 38.7 ns/element. MAGE explicitly notes that scalar 2D polynomial evaluation is too slow, mandating the use of separable or 1D Chebyshev approximations. This structural decision demonstrates a necessity: the mathematical formalism must be translated into kernels that are compatible with SIMD and low-cost computation, maintaining the hard-budgeted constraint [ (Issues)].

The L1 norm (or Total Variation distance in probabilistic terms) is utilized because it provides a tight, linear bound directly linked to the probability mass that was excluded from computation. While other metrics like the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence are widely used for comparing probability distributions , the L1 norm provides a superior foundation for a resource-constrained, hard-budgeted system like MAGE. The L1 bound is straightforward to calculate dynamically and directly enforce against a tolerance \varepsilon during the shortlist selection process.
4.2 The Tensor Algebraic Program (TAP) Parser and Bounded Latency
The TAP parser is the control mechanism that enforces the predictability mandate. It enforces a strict per-step operations budget (fixed at 42 operations across stages like feature encoding, predicates, and micro-contraction) to ensure predictable worst-case latency. The architecture mandates that the shortlist selection—identifying the high-weight slices for contraction—must be highly efficient, scaling as O(K \log S) (using heap or partial sort) and remaining robust even under adversarial data or large K (up to tens of thousands of slices) [ (Issues)].
TAP relies on the confidence metric \Delta = P_{i j^{\ast}} - P_{i j^{(2)}} to execute an early commit path if the confidence exceeds a predefined threshold \tau. This mechanism directly optimizes latency by avoiding unnecessary computation when the output uncertainty is low. However, as discussed in Section 2.3, the calibration of \tau is non-trivial; it must balance fast commitment speed against the risk of committing to a transient state in a slow-mixing Markov process. Validation protocols require measuring \Delta distributions under real workloads to ensure the adaptive \tau setting aligns with the desired accuracy and operational risk [ (Issues)].
4.3 Error Propagation in Fixed-Point Quantization
Quantization is necessary for efficient deployment, particularly for embedded or CPU-first integer kernels, and plays a crucial role in maintaining numerical determinism. MAGE implements binary-aligned quantization, where values are rounded to multiples of 2^{-q}. The resolution q is chosen based on the tolerable error \varepsilon via the relation q \ge -1 - \log_2(\varepsilon).
To counteract precision loss inherent in reduced-bit representations, MAGE mandates the use of 64-bit intermediates for fixed-point accumulation. This fixed-point accumulation strategy limits the magnitude of cumulative error, which scales as O(2^{-Q}), where Q is the number of fractional bits used.
Robust numerical safeguards are critical to prevent catastrophic arithmetic failure. This includes implementing clamping, explicit epsilon floors, and safe exponential/log approximations to guard against NaN/Inf leakage and catastrophic cancellation [ (Engineering constraints, Issues)]. All fixed-point decode paths must be tested in the presence of these safeguards to validate cumulative error boundaries [ (Issues)].
5. Guarantees of Reproducibility and Numerical Stability
5.1 The Fundamental Challenge: Floating-Point Non-Associativity (FPNA)
The most severe challenge to achieving "lossless" (bit-wise deterministic) projection in parallel systems is the inherent nature of floating-point arithmetic. Floating-point numbers, standardized by IEEE 754, introduce rounding errors because they cannot represent all real numbers precisely. Crucially, floating-point arithmetic is not associative; generally, (a + b) + c \neq a + (b + c).
In modern high-performance computing (HPC) environments, especially those utilizing massive parallelism (such as GPU matrix multiplication or CPU reductions), the order in which partial sums are accumulated is dynamically determined by factors like thread scheduling, block size, and kernel choice. Since accumulation order is variable, the results of summation and reduction operations will vary slightly across different runs, even with identical inputs and software stacks. These systematic differences, which stem from differences in kernel-level reduction behavior, manifest as bitwise-different outputs for mathematically identical operations. This instability prevents reproducibility, accurate sensitivity analysis, and ultimately prohibits system certification for high-stakes applications.
5.2 Mandatory Reduction Protocols for Determinism
MAGE's mandate for auditability requires the complete elimination of this run-by-run variability caused by FPNA. The solution is to strictly enforce a fixed computational graph for all reduction operations.
MAGE mandates the use of pairwise or fixed-order reduction patterns to guarantee identical outputs regardless of core count or scheduling, thereby actively guarding against race-mediated non-determinism. This strategy is supported by extensive research in numerical stability:
 * Reproducible Algorithms: Specialized algorithms like those implemented in ReproBLAS can compute a reproducible sum of floating-point numbers independent of the summation order, achieving significantly smaller error bounds than conventional summation methods. MAGE's use of 64-bit accumulation and pairwise reduction aligns with these high-precision strategies.
 * Vendor Constraints: Industrial tooling reinforces this approach. Libraries such as Intel oneAPI MKL offer Conditional Numerical Reproducibility (CNR) functionality, allowing developers to explicitly select a code branch (e.g., AVX2, AVX512) and enforce strict mode (MKL_CBWR="..."). This enforces fixed cache sizes, static scheduling, and deterministic reductions, ensuring reproducibility across hardware architectures. Similarly, frameworks like PyTorch offer deterministic options, although often at the cost of single-run performance.
The operational necessity here dictates a critical engineering trade-off: MAGE must deliberately reject high-throughput, non-deterministic kernel optimizations in favor of auditable, fixed-order kernels. By prioritizing guaranteed consistency and auditability over maximum single-run peak throughput, MAGE is positioned for use in high-stakes quantitative and certification environments where reproducible results are paramount.

6. The MAGE Architecture for Auditable HMC Computation (Synthesis)
6.1 Operationalizing Tiered Contraction
MAGE’s operational strategy is centered on lazy block contraction. Computation proceeds by operating on small B \times B output blocks, where B is typically chosen from \{32, 64\} such that 8 \cdot B^2 buffers fit within the L1/L2 caches, thereby maximizing cache locality and minimizing memory latency. Only the slices identified in the TAP shortlist are used to generate these blocks.
The architecture demands highly optimized kernel implementations for each tier:
 * Warm Tier Optimization: To achieve its latency target (\sim 7.1 ns/element), the Warm tier requires meticulous memory layout for its low-rank factors. The constraint to ensure V is transposed and packed guarantees contiguous memory access during inner loops, allowing for optimal SIMD unrolling and vectorization [ (Issues)].
 * Cold Tier Optimization: Since complex 2D polynomial evaluation is too computationally intensive, the Cold tier mandates mathematically equivalent, separable or 1D Chebyshev approximations [ (Issues)]. This constraint ensures that high-dimensional mathematics can be solved efficiently while adhering to budget constraints.
6.2 The Deterministic Production Roadmap
The MAGE roadmap ensures that determinism is engineered into the system from the foundational stages, rather than being patched later.
 * Phase 0 (Foundation): Establishes the core heap-based TAP shortlist (O(K log S)) and initiates the packed Warm inner loop implementation, along with baseline performance measurement and accuracy testing against dense baselines.
 * Phase 1 (Tuning): Focuses on hardening numerical stability, including float paths with double accumulation and the full implementation of the fixed-point quantization pipeline, with robust encode/decode functionality.
 * Phase 2 (Integration): This critical phase integrates parallel block contraction with mandatory deterministic reduction, along with the dynamic tier promotion/demotion policy. The TAP budget counters are also activated here to enforce operational constraints.
 * Phase 3 (Production): Emphasizes performance scaling, introducing SIMD intrinsics and embedded integer kernels, all while maintaining the deterministic mandate and deploying comprehensive monitoring dashboards.
The telemetry mandate—recording shortlist size, blocks generated, tier transitions, and latency—serves a dual purpose. Beyond simple monitoring, it provides the essential data stream required for the dynamic control system, enabling the adaptive adjustment of \tau thresholds and tuning tier promotion/demotion policies based on observed accuracy/cost trade-offs.

7. Conclusion: Synthesis of Theory and Production-Readiness
MAGE achieves the deterministic generalization of Markov Chains to higher dimensions by executing a controlled transition from the dense, intractable 2D stochastic matrix formalism to a sparse, tractable dD stochastic tensor contraction architecture. The system successfully reconciles the user's demand for "lossless" projection with the necessity of approximation imposed by the curse of dimensionality.
This reconciliation is achieved by implementing a mathematically rigorous framework of Controlled-Loss Projection. Tractability is guaranteed by operationalizing an optimized, tiered Tensor Network contraction engine (Hot/Warm/Cold), leveraging low-rank factorizations and separable kernel approximations to maintain polynomial complexity while bounding approximation error via the L1 omission bound \|P - \hat{P}\|_1 \le W_{\text{om}}.
Crucially, the system addresses the most significant hurdle in modern large-scale computation: the run-by-run variability caused by Floating-Point Non-Associativity (FPNA) in parallel environments. MAGE’s mandatory protocols—fixed RNG, fixed-order reduction trees, and 64-bit accumulation—ensure bit-wise identical outputs, validating the claim of auditability and numerical determinism across heterogeneous core counts and parallel schedulers.

 * Dynamic Policy Tuning: The system’s operational peak performance relies on the adaptive tuning of the TAP confidence threshold \tau. Future work must focus on linking this threshold to empirical ergodicity diagnostics, such as real-time estimation of the spectral gap or mixing time, to ensure that early commits balance latency goals against long-term system stability and convergence [ (Issues)].
 * Parallel Validation and Parity: While mandated, achieving proven deterministic parity across all multi-platform deployments remains a rigorous engineering task.  * Model Integration and Boundary Definition: As MAGE vectors are used as deterministic retrieval keys for context augmentation with typically non-deterministic LLMs, the boundary between MAGE's auditable output and the stochastic generation phase of the LLM must be precisely defined and instrumented via telemetry. MAGE’s deterministic nature provides a reliable anchor for debugging and validation in the otherwise highly variable LLM ecosystem.
